The question

What do we know, why do we believe it, and when should it change?

The idea, in everyday words

A belief is a fact that a person or a company treats as true. All work rests on beliefs. A claim is what a source says, before anyone has checked it. A belief is a claim the company has accepted.

An example. A courier company plans a route over a bridge. The plan rests on one belief: the bridge is open today. A driver's morning report is the source of that belief. The belief holds for today, and no longer. When the company hears that the bridge has closed, the belief changes, and the plan changes with it.

The question

Given uncertainty, what should we do, and what is timely information worth?

The idea, in everyday words

A forecast says what we expect to happen. A decision says what we will do about it. The desk keeps the two apart, because one is a claim about the world and the other is an act.

An example. A shopkeeper expects a hot weekend, so she orders ice. The forecast can be wrong, and the order still has to go in. Her cost comes in her own units: kilos of ice bought and left unsold. She sets a line in advance, and the order changes only when the forecast crosses it. After the weekend she compares what sold with what she expected, and keeps the score.

Timing matters too. A warning on Friday is worth more to her than the same warning on Saturday night.

The question

Who may propose, accept, decide and act?

The idea, in everyday words

Work splits into acts, and the acts do not carry the same authority. One person may prepare a proposal. Another may accept it. A third may carry it out. The rule holds for machines too: the model proposes, and a person accepts.

The question

How does an outcome change beliefs, methods and goals?

The idea, in everyday words

A forecast meets the world, and the world answers. The gap between the two is the score. A score on its own changes nothing, so the desk asks what caused it.

An example. A delivery company expects a quiet Monday, so it plans two vans. Monday is heavy, and parcels wait a day. The company measures the gap. Then it asks why: was the belief about Mondays wrong, did a signal arrive late, or was the plan itself wrong?

A miss can correct the plan or the belief about Mondays: that is the first loop. A miss that repeats can change the method, the way the company plans, or the goal behind it: that is the second loop.

Why a review desk exists

This chapter adds Decision questions on Gemini and Composition table. 17 of 18 components are in the story by now.

This chapter explains the problem that InGRID solves. It uses no technical words. If you read only one chapter, read this one.

The analyst's job

An energy analyst writes an outlook. An outlook is a forecast: how much power the grid will have, how much people will use, and what it will cost. Companies, planners and traders act on it.

Every outlook rests on assumptions. An assumption is one fact that the forecast takes as given. Three examples:

  • Demand peaks near the usual evening value.
  • PEDC Unit 2 in Visayas supplies 83.7 MW all day.
  • Both undersea links run as scheduled.

Each assumption can become wrong at any moment. A plant trips. A cable goes down for repair. A heat wave pushes demand up. The forecast still looks fine on paper, but one of its legs is gone.

What goes wrong today

The analyst reads the operator's notices, the market data and the news. Then the analyst tries to remember which forecast depends on which fact. That is hard for three reasons.

  1. The notices arrive all day, and most of them touch nothing the analyst cares about.
  2. The link from a notice to an assumption lives in the analyst's head, not in a system.
  3. When the analyst does nothing, nobody can tell a careful "no change" from a missed change.

The third reason matters most. A forecast that nobody reviewed and a forecast that someone reviewed and kept look the same. The desk makes them look different.

What InGRID does

InGRID is a review desk. It does four things, in order.

  1. It reads new evidence, such as an operator notice, and records when the evidence became known.
  2. It finds the assumption that the evidence touches.
  3. It asks an AI model a fixed set of questions about the evidence and the assumption. The model answers with citations. Code turns the answers into one label: supports, challenges, mixed, unrelated or not enough evidence.
  4. It lets the analyst decide: keep the assumption, change it, or open it for review. The decision and its reason go into the history.

The AI never decides. It does the reading and the comparing. The analyst keeps the authority. A kept assumption with a good reason counts as a success. That last sentence is the heart of the project, and the site says it on the screen.

What InGRID is not

  • It is not a forecast model. It does not predict prices or demand. The price outlook it keeps is a position, not a prediction. It states an expectation for a month, from a seasonal baseline. The desk issues it once, never edits it, and scores it later against what settled.
  • It is not a chatbot over PDF files. The questions never change, and the answers have fixed options.
  • It does not run the grid or send anything to the operator.
  • It does not claim that it prevents blackouts. We have no evidence for such a claim, so we do not make it.

Who decides whether an assumption stays or changes?

The AI reads and compares. The analyst decides and gives a reason. A kept assumption with a reason is a success.

Evidence, assumptions and cutoff times

This chapter adds Shared vocabulary profile v1 and Known-then compiler. 17 of 18 components are in the story by now.

This chapter explains the three ideas that make the desk honest about time. Everything else in the method builds on them.

Evidence has a clock

A piece of evidence is a source, a passage in it, and the moment it became known. The desk never drops the clock. NGCP posted the trip notice at 13:39 on 21 September 2026, five minutes after the trip itself. The return notice came at 15:55, 48 minutes after the unit came back. A person who read only the notices knew about the trip at 13:39, not at 13:34.

The desk also records where each passage came from, and whether a later notice only repeats an earlier one. Two notices that say the same thing are one fact, not two.

An assumption has a value, a time span and a source

The assumption in our case reads: "PEDC Unit 2 holds its registered maximum of 83.7 MW on 21 September 2026." It has an entity, a value with a unit, a day, and a source: the register. The desk accepted it at the start of the day. That acceptance is the first line of the history.

An assumption is a position the analyst holds, not a measured fact. The desk keeps the two apart. The trip does not prove the assumption false for the whole day. It proves that the day did not go as assumed.

The cutoff time

The desk judges a case at a cutoff time. At that moment it stops reading. Only evidence known before the cutoff may enter the judgment. The desk judges each new notice twice: one second before the notice became known, and one second after. The difference between the two judgments is the effect of that one notice.

The desk judges twice: just before the notice, and just after. Switch the cutoff.

Register, February 2026: PEDC Unit 2, maximum 83.7 MW. known since February
Notice V133937, posted 13:39: PEDC Unit 2 tripped at 13:34. known at 13:39
Notice V155556, posted 15:55: back on line at 15:07. known at 15:55, outside both cutoffs

Before the notice, the desk may read only the register. The answer should be: the assumption stands.

This pair is the core trick. It stops the AI from reading the future. When the desk asks about the trip, the model cannot see the return notice, because at 13:39 nobody could. It also gives the analyst a clean before-and-after view of every notice.

Why identity matters

A notice names a unit by a code or by a name. The register names it by another code. The desk keeps a reviewed table that joins them. If the table has no match, the judgment stops with "cannot tell", and no assumption changes. A fuzzy name match never counts as the same unit.

Eight fixed questions

The desk does not ask the model "what do you think?". It asks eight short questions, each with a fixed list of allowed answers.

  1. Which unit is this about?
  2. What kind of event is it?
  3. When did it happen, compared with the assumption's day?
  4. Is the statement conditional or planned?
  5. Does it only repeat an earlier source?
  6. Does it report a fact, not a plan?
  7. Is the evidence enough to decide?
  8. Taken alone, does it support or challenge the assumption?

Q2 is a choice question, so no form negates it. The same answer in every form gives a flip rate of 0, stable. Two different answers out of four would give 0.5, above the 0.25 threshold, and the label would become "not enough evidence".

Four presentation forms, one stability record

Language models can change their answer when the question changes shape. To catch that, the desk asks each question in four presentation forms inside one request. One form changes the order of the options. For a yes-or-no question, that form asks the question the other way round instead. Another form replaces the option names with numbers, and the last reverses the evidence passages.

The forms never rephrase a question. Some forms come out the same: with one passage, the reversed order changes nothing. If the forms agree, the stability record says "stable". When more than a quarter of a question's forms disagree with the majority, the judgment counts as unstable. Its label then becomes "not enough evidence". On the PEDC trip, every form agreed on every question.

Evidence or assumption

Known-at carries its basis. An evidenced known-at is a time the source itself stamps. Examples are the posting time of an operator message and the time of the team's own pull of a public series. Where no such stamp exists, known-at is an assumed bound, and the record names the assumption.

The impact of a version is the sum, over the hours, of the uncovered volume times the forecast price minus the contract price. What the studies of AI at work measure

Studies report that large language models touch a large share of job tasks, and that AI help raises output in writing and customer support.

#Abstract

We describe the design and first prototype, InGRID, of a decision desk for an electricity retailer in the Philippine Wholesale Electricity Spot Market, built so that every decision taken with AI assistance can later be traced to the evidence, the beliefs, the method and the person behind it. The paper reports what the prototype demonstrates and measures today, and states the hypotheses it does not yet test. On the market's own data, outage schedules matched to the system operator's advisories by event type, plant and unit appeared a median 8.5 hours after the advisory (27 matched rows of 148 late ones); 96.6% of 1,275 generator outage advisories in 2026 have no schedule row for the same plant within six hours of an outage start, a coverage gap rather than a measured delay. Two simple price challengers beat three baselines in 28 of 192 series–horizon–slice groups under a pre-stated gate, 27 at the day horizon; at the month horizon, where contracting decisions sit, 27 origins per series make a claim impossible under the gate, so no skill is claimed there. A deterministic warehouse query lists, for any price interval, the conditions present in six families with the time each was knowable, and ranks them by how unusual each was, labelled as evidence and never as cause; two cases are worked in full. A hosted language model answered a compiled set of eight bounded questions on one real outage under two information cutoffs, cited its passages, gave the same answers in four presentation forms of each question, and disagreed once with a rule-based reading; the disagreement is kept. We name the frameworks and models used, including TimesFM, TabFM, gradient-boosted classifiers and Gemini, and the theoretical role each fills. From the measurements we state a design hypothesis: that the value of AI in decision work is bounded by traceability and trust, that traceability is a property of records written at the time, and that trust must be earned by structure and then measured. We derive requirements from twelve stakeholder-supplied user stories and twelve desk questions, grade forty-four market factors by what is a record today, specify seven record kinds with one time contract, and specify a governed generative interface in which a model arranges record-backed components under a validator that rejects any figure not quoted from a record. No month has been scored, no analyst has used the prototype, and no effect on work has been measured. We report these gaps as results and give the experiments, with numerical thresholds, that would refute or narrow the design.

#1. Introduction

#1.1 The question

Who answers for a decision made with AI?

Organizations decide on beliefs about their world: what capacity exists, what a counterparty will do, which rule is in force, what a price will be. Large language models change who can do the reading. A model can read operator notices, filings and price data at a scale no desk can match, and draft a change to the plan for each. Organizations are now deciding whether, and how, to let it.

When that happens, the scarce thing is no longer reading. It is the answer to a different question: what did the organization believe on the day, on which evidence, who accepted it, and why? When an AI read the evidence and proposed the belief, that answer becomes harder to give, not easier. Field studies report that AI assistance raises output on many tasks [Brynjolfsson et al. 2025] and can lower quality on tasks just outside the model's competence [Dell'Acqua et al. 2026]. An organization that cannot tell the two apart after the fact cannot learn which reliance was appropriate [Lee and See 2004].

#1.2 The design hypothesis

We work from one hypothesis, stated here so that the paper can be read against it:

The value of AI in decision work is bounded by two properties, traceability and trust. Traceability is a property of records: it exists when every decision, belief, forecast and AI answer is written at the time, carries its times, and cannot be changed later. Trust is a property of people's reliance on the system, which structure can earn but only measurement can confirm.

A better model does not, by itself, let a board see who decided what on which evidence. A record written at the time does. This paper demonstrates the record half: a schema, an implementation, and measurements of the latencies that make time fields necessary. The trust half is not measured here; Section 12 states how it would be. The claim therefore has two statuses: the traceability properties are demonstrated on the prototype, and the bound on value is a hypothesis.

The problem has three levels, each with a different reader (Table 1).

#1.3 What we build and what we report

We design an AI-assisted decision desk and build its first prototype for one retailer in one market. The prototype is InGRID (Interpretable Grid Reasoning for Informed Decisions): a review desk that records each step from evidence to claim to assumption to decision against a grid ontology, so that every decision can be answered for. Its assistant persona, Ingrid, proposes a position and shows her working; the person accepts, changes or rejects it, and the record says which. The research object is the record structure that makes a decision answerable, and the research question is whether that structure can be built and operated on real market data at the latencies the data imposes. At the time of writing, the prototype's language-model component has run on one real case under two cutoffs, and no analyst has used the desk. The record kinds are implemented and tested; the step that scores an issued forecast against settled prices is implemented and waits for the first settled month.

#1.4 Contributions

  1. A problem statement for AI-assisted decision work framed as accountability rather than capability, with one time contract and five falsifiable propositions (Section 2).
  2. A prototype on a real market, with its data, its architecture, two fully worked cases of the driver card, the alert record, the recorded judgment, and the status of every record kind (Section 3).
  3. A methods-and-models account: each framework and model used, the record it produces, the theory it instantiates and its measured result (Section 4).
  4. Candidate requirements from twelve stakeholder-supplied user stories, translated into twelve desk questions with the parts every answer must show (Section 5), and a watch list of forty-four market factors graded by what is a record today (Section 6, Appendix B).
  5. Graded preliminary measurements, including negative and null results and the numbers the hypothesis depends on that do not yet exist (Sections 7 and 8).
  6. A framework of seven record kinds and their invariants, grounded in four established theories (Section 9), and a specified governed generative interface in which a model arranges record-backed components under a validator (Section 10).
  7. An evaluation plan with a numerical threshold that would refute or narrow each claim (Section 12).

#1.5 Scope and terms

The prototype supports market intelligence and analyst decisions. It is not a causal model of the market, a trading system or a grid control system. We claim no forecast skill beyond the groups reported and no effect on productivity. Every portfolio-impact figure in pesos comes from a synthetic portfolio and demonstrates a method; the price and error figures are real. We say belief throughout for a proposition a forecast rests on; the prototype's own register files call the same record an assumption. InGRID names the prototype as a whole, and Ingrid its assistant persona, which proposes and never decides. Evidence grades are defined in Section 7.1 and used in every table.

#2. Problem formulation

#2.1 One time contract

Every record in the design carries the time fields of Table 2. The same names are used in every section.

Where the data carries no receipt of first availability, the known-at field is an assumed bound, not an observed earliest availability, and the fact names the assumption it rests on. The three assumptions, as implemented in the warehouse: A-RT, a real-time market value is treated as available at the end of the five-minute interval it describes; A-RUN, a projected outage-schedule record is treated as available at the time of the first daily snapshot in which the record appears, because the schedule files carry no run timestamp (so the bound can be later than the true availability, never earlier); A-FUEL, a public fuel or exchange-rate value is treated as available after a fixed lag per publisher: two days after the period for the daily oil series, one day for the daily exchange rates, and the fifteenth of the following month for the monthly coal and LNG series. An evidenced known-at, by contrast, is a time the source itself stamps: the posting time of an operator message, or the time of our own pull of a public series. The two fields are stored separately and a replay in strict mode reads only the evidenced one.

#2.2 Objects

A signal s is an item of evidence with its known-at time tks and its valid time tvs. A belief b is a proposition with an owner, a provenance layer (official, analytical, inferred, exploratory, hypothesis), a source, a valid interval [vfromb, vtob) and an epistemic state (accepted, unverified, contradicted). The accepted snapshot at cutoff t is

B(t) = { b : stateb(t) = accepted, vfromb ≤ t < vtob },

computed only from records whose recorded-at time is before t, or, for evidence collected after the fact, whose known-at time is before t and is so marked.

A position, here a price forecast, is a version Pv = fm(B(tcv), Xv) with cutoff tcv, inputs Xv fixed by content digest, beliefs fixed by identifier, and a method m with its version. Versions are superseded, never edited.

The impact of a position on a portfolio with uncovered volume qh in hour h and contract price c is

Iv = ∑h ∈ H qh (p̂v,h − c),

kept as a band over the forecast quantiles, with its sign. A change is material when |Iv' − Iv| ≥ τ, where the line τ is stated before any change is scored.

A decision records what was decided, on which position version and snapshot, by whom, when, and why. A scoring receipt compares Pv with the settled truth y under a stated loss L(Pv, y).

#2.3 Attribution: what is implemented and what is proposed

Implemented. An attribution is a record written by a person beside a scoring receipt. It names the receipt and the forecast version by digest, states a cause of one of three kinds (a belief by identifier, a signal by source and times, or the method), gives a statement, and may give a share in [0, 1]. Nothing in the prototype computes an attribution.

Proposed. Let K be the set of accepted beliefs that changed between the version used, v, and a later version v'. For k ∈ K, let Pv(k) be the forecast re-run with belief k alone set to its later value. The computed attribution of the miss to k is

Ak = L(Pv, y) − L(Pv(k), y).

A miss is a scored version whose loss exceeds a threshold stated with the loss (for this prototype, the best baseline's loss on the same month). A miss is avoidably attributable to k if Ak > 0 and the later value of k was knowable, under the time contract, before the cutoff tcv; a belief learned only after the cutoff explains the miss but does not make it avoidable. Two measures follow and must not be confused. Attribution coverage is the share of scored misses that carry a person-written attribution; it needs only the implemented record and a settled month. The avoidable-miss share is the number of avoidably attributable misses over the number of scored misses in a month; it needs the computed Ak and a join from the belief's later value to the time that value became knowable, neither of which is implemented. A person's optional share field establishes neither the counterfactual improvement nor the knowability. One-at-a-time re-runs are a first-order approximation; interacting belief changes would need a joint re-run, which we do not propose here. This is a statement about the organization's own beliefs, not a causal claim about the market. The same discipline governs the desk's reading of the market: a factor present at a time is listed and ranked by how unusual it was, never named as the cause (Section 3.4).

#2.4 Five propositions

#3. The domain and the prototype

#3.1 Why this market

An electricity retailer in the Philippine wholesale market buys power at nodal spot prices and sells it to customers, partly under fixed-price contracts. Its board must decide what to contract, what to leave exposed and when to act. The market was created by the Electric Power Industry Reform Act of 2001 [Republic of the Philippines 2001] and runs under rules the energy department promulgated in 2002 and has amended since [DOE 2002; DOE 2016]. A reserve market was declared by circular in 2023 [DOE 2023] and commenced full commercial operations on 26 January 2024 under a department advisory [IEMOP 2024]. The regulator approves the price determination methodology. The two market operators applied in January 2023 for its extension to reserves; the regulator granted interim relief to adopt and implement the proposed methodology in an order of 24 August 2023, and issued further orders on its implementation in 2024, among them a suspension of part of the settlement manual on 25 March 2024 and a partial-settlement directive on 9 May 2024 [ERC 2023; NGCP 2024]. The orders were not retrieved from the regulator's own site; the chronology is read from the transmission operator's customer bulletin, which quotes them. The regulator also sets administered prices when the market is suspended, as it did from 26 March 2026 under a national energy emergency [ERC 2026a; ERC 2026b]. The information a retailer needs exists but arrives scattered: operator advisories, outage schedules, registrations, project lists, rule changes, administered-pricing notices and the price series. The evidence is public and time-stamped, the rules come from a regulator, and the truth settles every billing month, so every forecast can be scored. Most demonstrations of AI at work cannot be scored; this one can.

#3.2 Data and availability

The operating company's copy of the market data holds 80 source families (3,357 files, about 40.0 GB) and, since a backfill on 17 October 2026, ends on 14 October 2026: five-minute intervals to 23:55 that day and operator advisories to 15 October. Final regional settlement prices run from billing month July 2021 to September 2026. Final nodal settlement prices, as exported before the backfill, cover 59 billing months, with the number of nodes growing from 887 to 1,214. A billing month runs from the 26th to the 25th; prices are five-minute, in pesos per MWh; timestamps are local times whose zone is not recorded. Administered pricing covers all of billing month April 2026, the month of the regulator's market suspension and modified administered price [ERC 2026a], and part of five other months; the meaning of the pricing flags in the data is inferred, not confirmed. A curated fuel table holds 8,365 rows of public series: a coal driver (Australian thermal coal, monthly), an LNG driver (the Japan import average, monthly, labelled as not the spot marker) and background series for crude oil and exchange rates.

Data availability. The market data is the operating company's licensed copy of the market operator's publications. A signed permission record of 13 October 2026, extended on 14 October 2026, covers its use in this work and the public display of regional prices, demand, reserves, dispatch and advisory text on the prototype. The bulk copy, the company's portfolio and the plant-name alias table are not redistributable and are held outside the code. Synthetic substitutes exist for every record kind so that the code and tests run without them, and every synthetic record is labelled as such.

#3.3 Architecture

The warehouse layer is the market's shared vocabulary made queryable: 2,178 entities (three regions, 323 plants, 549 generating units, 1,288 market resources, two inter-island links, four commodities and nine series references) and 3,543 relations drawn from a closed list of six (located in, part of, represents, uses fuel, connects, refers to). The list is a schema constraint: a new relation requires a new schema version. A causes relation is not in the list. Every fact carries its valid time, its made-at time where the source states one, and its known-at time with its basis.

#3.4 Applied result 1: the driver card, two worked cases

For a region and a time, the driver card lists the conditions present in six families, demand (D), supply (S), price (P), outages (O), fuel (F) and rules, each fact with its value, unit, source and known-at time, in one of the three modes of Table 2. Operator messages name plants in free text. A message is linked to a plant (not a unit) only through an accepted alias: either a manually reviewed entry, or one accepted by a deterministic rule batch that admits a plant's own node or prefix code, a word or word pair of the registered facility name, or initials of three or more characters, and rejects a token that is only a word of the company name, names a plant absent from the registration list, names another plant in the region, or appears in a message stating more than 1.1 times the plant's registered capacity. Of 1,608 aliases, 1,332 were accepted (covering 40,129 mentions) and 276 left uncertain; an uncertain match is listed as unconfirmed and never counted.

A ranking orders the demand, supply, outage and fuel families. For demand, supply and outages the indicator is compared with the same region at the same clock hour on the 28 days before the time (the time itself excluded), using only facts the mode admits; the score is the fraction of those reference values below the indicator, counting ties as one half. The indicators are: demand, the regional requirement in MW; supply, the larger score among the reserve shortfall in MW, the import share of requirement and the number of congested inter-island links; outages, the larger score among two indicators: the scheduled-out capacity in MW, summed over the schedule records visible in the mode (in the assumed and strict modes, records not yet superseded at the cutoff; in hindsight, the latest revision), one row per resource with the four projection runs collapsed to the largest capacity, and the derated capacity in MW named in operator messages linked through accepted aliases, taking the total in the latest such message posted in the six hours ending at the interval's end. A family with no visible fact scores "no data" rather than zero; a region with no visible schedule record at all scores "no data" for scheduled outages. Fuel is scored differently: its driver series (coal and LNG) are compared against their own full history, because fuel is a slow-moving cost level and not an intra-day move, and whenever any non-fuel family scores 0.8 or more, fuel is placed after every scored non-fuel family whatever its own score (in the second worked case this puts demand at 0.426 ahead of fuel at 0.507). A family's score is its strongest indicator; ties between families fall to family order. The price is shown as context and not scored; rules flags are listed present or absent, never scored. The ranking's heading is fixed: likely drivers, ranked by how unusual each was against the last 28 days at this hour; evidence, not proof.

Case 2 shows what the time contract buys. In hindsight the 150 MW outage dominates; at the time, the schedule rows that record it did not yet exist, so the assumed-mode card ranks the outage family first on a smaller derating signal; the strict-mode card for case 2 was not recorded. A desk that reads only the hindsight card would overstate what was knowable. Case 1's strict-mode result, "no data" in every family, is the honest statement that almost nothing about that interval carried a receipt of first availability; it is a measurement of the data, not of the market.

#3.5 Applied result 2: the alert record

When the price in a region crosses a threshold an authenticated user has set, the desk writes one alert with five fields: the price and threshold; a severity line as in Table 4; the ranked likely drivers under the fixed heading; a relationship trail, which is the chain of entities and facts the ranking rests on (for case 1: the unit's trip message, its plant, the region; the import share over the congested inter-island link; the demand against its median; the price); and a list of what to check next (the unit's return time in later messages and the schedule; the link's congestion in the next intervals; demand against its median). If the user has entered a portfolio, the alert carries the impact of that interval on the uncovered volume, labelled with the portfolio version and effective date; otherwise it says "Not available: no portfolio entered". Portfolio values are held in a private store under the user's sign-in and never enter the public data files. The alert for case 1 was run and recorded; no live alert has been sent to a user.

#3.6 Applied result 3: the recorded judgment

On case 1, the trip of PEDC unit 2 (Panay Energy Development Corporation, Visayas) reported at 13:34 on 21 September 2026 and its return at 15:07, the compiled question set of Section 9.3 ran twice on a hosted Gemini model under two cutoffs, once after the trip advisory and once after the return advisory (Section 7.5; the passages, questions, thresholds and rules are in Appendix C). Both runs cited their passages and were stable across the four presentation forms of Section 9.3. At the return cutoff the model's composed label disagrees with a rule-based reading of the same passages. The disagreement is recorded on the event page and not yet adjudicated by an analyst.

#3.7 Status of each record kind

#4. Methods and models

The prototype uses several frameworks and models. Each fills one role in the framework of Section 9, produces one record kind, and is held to one theory. None of them is the record; the record is what a person can later open. Table 6 lists them.

Three roles follow from the table. Forecasting models produce positions and must beat the baselines under a proper loss before any claim; the foundation models are challengers on the same footing and one of them lost. Classifiers produce signals whose probability is an event probability, kept apart from any semantic score. The language model proposes belief changes and arranges views; it never writes a belief, a decision or a figure. Vendor products appear where a reader needs the name to reproduce the work; nothing in the framework depends on the vendor.

#5. Requirements from target users

#5.1 Twelve stakeholder-supplied user stories

The operating company supplied twelve user stories, written by one of its market staff for traders, portfolio managers and risk staff and relayed to us without an elicitation protocol. They are candidate requirements, not the result of a user study. The stories describe outcomes and rarely mention AI; their shared demand is decisions the users can trace and defend. Table 7 maps each story to the records that serve it and to a verdict: adopt (the design serves it as stated), reframe (the design serves it in a narrower form) or defer (outside the current design, with the reason).

Two reframings matter. Stories 2 and 10 ask for a "driving factor" and "root causes". The design answers with two records: the ranking of what was unusual at the time, labelled evidence and not proof, and the attribution of a miss to the organization's own beliefs. It does not claim what moved the market; that would need a causal model of the market, which is outside scope. Story 12 is the bridge between the users and the headline question: a risk manager who can trace every alert to its sources, logic and model version can answer for a decision made with AI.

#5.2 Twelve desk questions and their mandatory parts

The desk's unit of work is the question a person asks. Each kind has parts every answer must show, so that an answer cannot omit the uncomfortable part; the rule governs fixed pages today and generated views in Section 10. Kinds 1 to 7 are answered by the warehouse views; 8 to 12 by the desk's pages and records.

The role flows are chains of kinds: a trader's morning is Q8, then Q1 on anything unusual, then Q2 to Q4; an alert is Q11, then Q1, then Q10; a portfolio manager's event is Q9, then Q10, then a decision; a risk manager's trace is Q7 applied to any answer plus its receipt; a risk analyst's month end is Q12.

#6. The watch list: market factors graded by what is a record

The same staff member listed forty-four factors in ten groups that the desk watches: supply fundamentals, demand fundamentals, the fuel market, renewable energy, reserve margins, weather, transmission, policy and regulation, market structure, and external and macroeconomic conditions. Appendix B grades every factor. On this desk a factor is never a cause. It enters as a signal, a condition present at a time with the time it was known, or as a belief the forecast rests on. One rule governs the list: a factor that is not a record cannot appear in an answer; the answer says "no record".

The grades separate three things that are often run together. Data availability: record (a curated table with a reader and a counted row set), in store, unread (a source table the company holds that no reader yet parses), public source, not pulled, documents, not tabulated, or absent. Validity: whether the quantity has an agreed definition; a factor is derivable when the records exist but the definition is not yet fixed. Scope: excluded marks a factor the study leaves out on purpose, with the reason. Table 9 summarizes by family; the per-factor grades are in Appendix B.

The reading: the desk can already show the outage, fuel, rules-flag and link families and actual demand; the supply quantities a trader names first are derivable but undefined, and some values are licence-restricted in public; reserve detail is in store but unread; weather and holidays have no record, so the 28-day same-hour reference used by the ranking treats a holiday like any other day. Where the list is thin, an answer says so rather than fill the gap with prose.

#7. Preliminary measurements

#7.1 Evidence grades

Every figure and status in this paper carries one grade. Measured: scored against labels or outcomes. Demonstrated: run on real data with a receipt, not scored against an outcome. Implemented: code and tests, exercised on test data only. Specified: a design with no run. Absent: no record.

#7.2 Signal timeliness (measured)

When the system operator posts an outage advisory, how long until its own outage schedule, the record a planner would use as a belief, shows the outage?

Protocol. The 2026 outage schedule rows (475 distinct rows covering 174 generating resources) were split into rows first seen before their outage started and rows first seen after (148 late rows). Each late row was matched to an outage advisory of the same event type (trip, forced or planned outage), the same plant and, where the row names a unit, the same unit, within six hours either way of the outage start. An earlier pass matched on free-text tokens and admitted unit mismatches; it found 39 matches and a median lag of 20.7 hours. The typed pass reported here found 27.

Result. For the 27 matched late rows the schedule first showed the outage a median 8.5 hours (508 minutes) after the advisory, 20.6 days at the 90th percentile, more than a day later in 12 of 27, and never before the advisory. Of 1,275 generator outage advisories in 2026, 1,232 (96.6%) have no schedule row for the same plant within six hours of any outage start. This second number is a coverage gap: it counts advisories the schedule does not reflect at all under the matching rule, and says nothing about how late the reflected ones were. Of the 121 unmatched late rows, 43 belong to plants with no outage advisory at all in 2026, and 76 of the remaining 78 are marked planned, so the schedule and the advisory feed largely describe different populations: planned outages that the schedule carries and the operator does not announce, and trips that the operator announces and the schedule rarely records. Case 1's trip has no schedule row at all. Measured from the outage start instead of the advisory, the late rows first appear a median 48 hours later.

Milestones. The energy department's committed-projects list "as of July 2026" shows a 350 MW coal unit with testing targeted for February 2026 and commercial operation for August 2026, status "ongoing testing and commissioning". The market data shows the same unit registered at 315 MW on 16 May 2026, dispatching above zero from 18 May, and dispatched in 8,927 of 8,928 five-minute intervals of the September 2026 billing month. The two sources do not contradict each other: dispatch during testing is consistent with a testing status. They do show that a planner who reads only the list would not know the unit was already delivering energy, and that whether test dispatch counts as available supply is a belief that needs an owner. We draw no latency claim from this comparison; a milestone slip would need two editions of the list, and the department's page keeps only the latest.

Reading. The schedule lags the advisory by hours where it reflects it, and does not reflect most trips. These are measurements of latency and coverage, not of value: they do not show that any decision lost money because of the lag.

#7.3 Price forecast: baselines and challengers (measured)

Protocol. Three baselines were scored on 16 series (four regional load-weighted prices and twelve de-identified load nodes of the retailer's sample) at day, week and month horizons, with an information cutoff 24 hours before each forecast origin and no filled gaps. Administered-pricing months form their own slice; months with mixed pricing are excluded. Two challengers, a ridge regression (alpha 100 on standardized features) and a histogram gradient-boosted regressor (40 trees, depth 3, learning rate 0.08, 16 bins, seed 0), use fourteen features computed only from rows that ended before the origin's cutoff: six circular calendar encodings of the target hour, a weekend flag, the same clock hour one week earlier and how many weeks back it was read, the means of the last 24 and 168 hours, their normalized difference, and the means of that clock hour over the last 7 and 28 days. A feature whose window is too sparse is refused and the origin is recorded as unserved; nothing is imputed. The challengers were fitted in six blocked walk-forward folds with a one-day embargo; the first fold's history is withheld, so 317 day, 305 week and 10 month origins per regional series are unserved and the challenger is compared with the baseline on the common origins only (for Luzon day, 1,246 of 1,254). The six folds begin, for the regional series, at 11 July 2022, 13 February 2023, 16 September 2023, 18 April 2024, 19 January 2025 and 22 August 2025; the models of a fold are fitted once on the settled days before it and forecast only that fold's origins. The 10 to 90% band is a fan of the challenger's own signed residuals on earlier settled windows, admitted only once their window ended at least one embargo before the origin. A group is a series × horizon × slice × challenger, 192 in all. A group claims skill only if all six rules hold: it beats the best of the three baselines (the one with the lowest mean absolute error on the common origins) on both mean absolute and root mean squared error; the gain's 95% interval from a week-block bootstrap (1,000 resamples of whole weeks of origins) excludes zero; the gain is positive in at least two thirds of the folds that hold scored origins; the band covers between 60% and 99% of outcomes overall and at least 55% in two thirds of the folds, where a fold counts toward calibration only if it holds at least 60 origins and insufficient evidence counts as failure; the common origins keep at least 95% of the record's scored origins; and the group is not on the administered slice. The calibration rule has a structural consequence stated here so the reader does not misread the month results: the month horizon has 27 origins per series in total, so no month fold reaches 60 origins and no month group can pass the gate whatever its forecasting quality.

Result. 28 of 192 groups claim skill: 27 at the day horizon (7 of 8 regional, 20 of 24 nodal) and one at the week horizon (one node, boosted trees, gain +136.0 with a lower bound of +19.6); none on the administered slice; and none at the month horizon, where the gate cannot be passed. At the week horizon the regional gains that exist (up to +59.7) sit inside the bootstrap interval. At the month horizon seven of eight regional groups have a positive point gain (ridge: +45.4 Luzon, +172.6 Visayas, +23.8 Mindanao, +90.7 system; boosted trees: +68.0, +32.4, −175.5, +82.3 pesos per MWh), every bootstrap interval spans zero (for example Visayas ridge [−205.7, +527.2]), fold gains change sign, and no fold is calibration-eligible. A later data backfill of the exports left the claim count at 28.

Reading. The scorecard shows weak methods as weak, as the electricity-price forecasting literature says a fair test must [Weron 2014; Lago et al. 2021]. Skill is established at the day horizon and, for one node, the week horizon. At the month horizon, where a retailer's contracting decisions sit, three things are true at once and must be kept apart: the observed gains are mostly positive, their statistical uncertainty is too wide to exclude zero on 27 origins, and the pre-stated gate makes a claim structurally impossible with so few origins. We therefore say that no month-horizon claim is possible under this gate, not that the horizon has no skill; either way, decisions there will rest on beliefs rather than on a demonstrated model.

#7.4 Beliefs, forecast versions and materiality (demonstrated; impact on synthetic data)

The belief register holds 42 beliefs. By provenance layer: 9 are fixed by an official document or the frozen method definition, 14 are analytical (measured from the data), 5 are inferred (an unconfirmed meaning), 9 are exploratory design choices and 5 are the price and rule beliefs the forecast versions rest on. By epistemic state: 2 are accepted by a person, 40 are unverified. By deployment: 26 are in force in the prototype's code (23 numeric thresholds and the 3 price beliefs), 14 (the composition rules of Section 9.3) have been exercised only on synthetic test cases, and 2 (the rule beliefs) are accepted. No threshold has been confirmed as an official market constant; the price cap and floor, for example, are the observed extremes of one month.

Forecast versions. Two versions of a system price forecast for billing month November 2026 (26 October to 25 November 2026) were issued, on 16 and 17 October 2026, with cutoffs 25 August and 25 September 2026, from the seasonal baseline; both rest on the five price and rule beliefs by identifier and on their input digests. They will be scored when the market operator's final settlement prices for that month are published, expected in December 2026. Between the two versions none of the five beliefs changed.

Materiality. The impact formula of Section 2.2 was run on a synthetic portfolio of twelve nodes with invented volumes and contract prices, against the baseline bands. The materiality line was fixed at 1,000,000 pesos per change before any input was read, and the procedure refuses to classify a change without a stated line. A change is the movement of the band median between consecutive forecast origins of one series. 19,468 of 82,080 changes (23.7%) are material, in 12 of 12 nodes: day 7,083 of 45,828; week 12,282 of 36,144; month 103 of 108. These are movements of the forecast, not changes of an accepted belief; the stronger test, that a change to an accepted belief moves the impact past the line at least once in a scored month, cannot run until a belief changes.

Reading. The line filters: three changes in four would not reach a decision-maker. Whether the line is right is a business question a synthetic portfolio cannot answer.

#7.5 AI judgment on a real case (demonstrated)

One experiment, two inference runs, four presentation forms of each question in each run. On 14 October 2026 the compiled question set of Section 9.3 ran on a hosted Gemini model (gemini-3.8-flash, thinking budget pinned to zero, JSON output) on case 1, the trip of PEDC unit 2 in the Visayas on 21 September 2026 at 13:34: once at the cutoff after the trip advisory (13:39:37; one passage; 9,743 tokens in, 1,784 out, 11.1 s) and once after the return advisory (15:55:56; four passages; 11,609 in, 1,776 out, 11.9 s). The passages, the atomic answers, the request digests and the composition rules are in Appendix C. The belief under test is the unit's available capacity of 83.7 MW, read from the operator's registered-capacity list; the list gives a registered value, not an observation on the event day, which is why the belief is a hypothesis to test rather than a fact. Both runs composed challenges by the rule "outage or later" (event read as a forced outage, time relation "same") and were stable across the four presentation forms. At the return cutoff a rule-based reading of the same four passages, which reads the set by its latest statement ("re-online at 1507H"), composes supports by the rule "availability confirmed"; the model read the event for the set as a forced outage. The disagreement is recorded and not yet adjudicated. No analyst decision exists at either cutoff: the case fixture commits none and leaves the decision to the person reviewing the case.

#8. Negative and null results

Compiled judgment versus a single question (measured on test data). Twenty-five test pairs, each a set of passages paired with a belief and a frozen expected label, were written by the authors; three pairs compile at two cutoffs, giving 28 compiled tasks, 18 in a calibration split and 10 in a test split. Each task was answered with no model call by a scripted replay of the frozen answers, under four conditions. The eight-question compiled set and a single direct question both scored 22 of 28. On the test split the single question scored 8 of 10 and the compiled set 7 of 10. The compiled set abstained four times, once correctly; at matched coverage, the 24 tasks every condition answers, its selective accuracy was 21 of 24 against 19 of 24. Every answer was stable across presentation forms, so the stability receipt's predictive value cannot be identified from this set. Thresholds were not tuned.

Reserve-shortfall onset models (measured). On the single time-ordered split the locally trained one-hour classifier reached a September test AUC of 0.914 (Visayas) and 0.950 (Mindanao) against an hour-of-day climatology of 0.87 and 0.82, on one seed. In a separate test, the rolling three-hour variant does not warn earlier than the operator: flagging the top 5% of its scores over June to September in the Visayas, it flagged 43 of 71 alert days before the alert started, with a median lead of three hours, but only 7 of them before the operator's own notice (which precedes an alert by a median 9.5 hours), and it flagged 20 of 49 days without an announced alert, most of them real reserve deficiencies the operator did not announce. The tabular foundation model tied it; the time-series foundation model, zero shot, was not competitive (Table 6).

Month-horizon price skill. No claim under this gate (Section 7.3): with 27 origins per series the calibration rule cannot be met, so the observed month gains are reported without a skill claim.

#9. Framework

#9.1 Four layers of responsibility

#9.2 Seven record kinds

Every record is validated, immutable and addressed by its content. Each kind rests on one tradition: the signal on bitemporal data, whose valid time is our valid time and whose transaction time is our recorded-at time [Jensen and Snodgrass 1999; Snodgrass 2000], and whose source trail follows the entity, activity and agent model of provenance [W3C 2013]; the belief on truth maintenance and belief revision; the position on forecasting; the impact on decision theory; the decision on coordination and authority; the scoring receipt on proper scoring and single-loop learning; the AI judgment on trust in automation. Known-at is our own field: it is neither valid nor transaction time, and it carries its basis because the sources rarely state when they became available.

#9.3 The AI as worker, never as the record

The model reads signals under the cutoff and proposes, for each, which belief it touches, what the change would be and why, with citations. It never decides. Its answer enters the belief set only through a person's recorded decision, which is kept. AI output cannot grant a role, approve a change or authorize execution; these are state transitions enforced in code and tested, not instructions to a model.

The compiled question set. For a passage set and a belief, the model answers eight bounded questions, each with a closed option set: (1) identity, which reviewed entity the passage refers to, or "cannot tell"; (2) event, which of planned outage, forced outage, return to service, derating, grid alert, testing, commercial operation or other; (3) time relation of the stated time to the belief's time, before, same, after or not stated; (4) conditional, whether the statement is conditional, planned or subject to clearance; (5) lineage, whether the passage repeats an earlier source already in context; (6) observation, whether it reports something that happened rather than a target; (7) sufficiency, whether the passages suffice to decide; (8) direction, whether, taken alone, it supports, challenges or leaves the belief unchanged. Each question is rendered in four presentation forms inside one request: the canonical form; a form that permutes the option order (for a yes/no question, a negated wording whose answer is un-negated before composition); a form that replaces the option labels with bracketed numbers, applied to the four choice questions only, so that for the four yes/no questions this form is identical to the canonical one; and a form that reverses the order of the passages. The question text is the same in every form except the negated yes/no form. The forms test sensitivity to presentation order, option labelling and negation, not to rephrasing. The count of distinct presentations depends on the task: with two or more passages a choice question has four and a yes/no question three; with a single passage the reversed-order form equals the canonical one, so a choice question has three and a yes/no question two. The recorded run at the trip cutoff (Section 7.5) read one passage, so its yes/no questions were tested on two distinct presentations and its choice questions on three; the run at the return cutoff read four passages and had the full count. The answer to a question is the modal top option across the forms; for a yes/no question each form's top is "yes" when its un-negated probability is at least 0.5, and an even split resolves to "no". The flip rate is the share of forms whose top differs from the mode, and the polarity gap is the absolute difference between the un-negated yes probabilities of the canonical and the negated form. A question with a flip rate above 0.25 or a polarity gap above 0.3 is unstable, and the set composes "insufficient" before any rule reads it. The numeric thresholds the rules name (sufficiency 0.6, conditional 0.5, lineage 0.5) are compared against the modal answer read as 1.0 for "yes" and 0.0 for "no", never against a probability, so each is in effect a test of the modal answer; all five values are untuned initial settings recorded in every receipt. Fourteen fixed rules, evaluated in the order given in Appendix C, then compose one label from the modal answers: supports, challenges, mixed, unrelated or insufficient. Among them: an unknown identity or an insufficient set composes insufficient; an entity other than the belief's composes unrelated; a grid alert names no unit and composes unrelated; testing on an availability belief composes challenges; an outage, derating or a later time composes challenges; a confirmed return or commercial operation at the same time composes supports; an earlier observation composes supports, or mixed when the direction answer disagrees; a conditional statement composes mixed; a time not stated composes mixed or insufficient; and a set no rule matches composes insufficient. The rule-based comparator in Section 7.5 applies the same fourteen rules to answers written by a person from the passages' latest statement.

#9.4 The scoring and attribution loop

#9.5 Invariants

  • Observations, attributed claims, accepted beliefs and simulation results are separate records.
  • Nothing is read before its known-at time, by a person or by a model.
  • Unknown identity, time or approval stays unknown; an ambiguous mapping cannot change a belief.
  • Proposal, analyst review, execution approval and execution receipt are distinct.
  • A semantic judgment score is never an event probability, and the two are never averaged.
  • A justified decision not to change is a success: a decision is judged by what was known, not by its outcome [Howard and Abbas 2016].
  • A change to an accepted belief marks the positions that used it, without erasing them.

#10. Governed generative views (specified)

The users ask to explore: to trace an event to the portfolio, to ask why an alert fired, to put a question in their own words (stories 4, 5, 6 and 10). A fixed dashboard answers the questions its designer foresaw. A generative interface lets a model compose the view that fits the question; the capability is now in research systems [Cao, Jiang and Xia 2025; Leviathan et al. 2026] and in a consumer assistant [Ropek 2026]. Two motivations drive our design and both are hypotheses to test: that a composed view lets a person explore faster than a fixed page, and that a composed view invites more reliance than its content deserves, as automation-bias research would predict for any polished automated output [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010]. Everything in this section has the grade specified: a contract without a run.

The catalogue. A component is a deterministic, record-backed part with a fixed query: a signal card (an advisory with its times and the units it names), a timeline of what the operator reported after an event, a chart of demand, price, flows and reserve around a time, a rules-flags card, a belief card, a forecast-versions card, an impact band, a judgment card with its stability receipt, a decision form and history, a finding (a rule's own one-sentence reason for a threshold crossing), a driver-card family block with the mode switch, the likely-drivers ranking, the alert record, an exposure table by hour and contract, a data-provenance badge (dataset, row count, licence) and the receipt itself. Each component declares its identifier, the query it may call, the argument schema (record identifiers, a region, a time, a cutoff, a mode: never a free value), the record kinds it reads, the mandatory parts it satisfies and whether it may be shown without sign-in.

Six rules govern the design.

  1. The model arranges; the records fill. The model chooses and orders components from the catalogue. A deterministic query fills every figure. The model's output contains no figure.
  2. The model plans over a manifest, never over values. For a case, a deterministic step lists what exists: component identifiers, record identifiers, finding identifiers, the ranking's labels, data states, the cutoff and mode. The validator rejects any component or query outside the catalogue, any argument outside the manifest, and any numeral in the model's text that is not a record field quoted by identifier. A model can still emit an unsupported figure; the validator refuses the plan that contains it, and the refusal is recorded.
  3. Every view is an AI judgment with a receipt. A generated view is record kind 7 applied to presentation. Its receipt holds the question and its wordings, the plan, the parts considered and left out with reasons, the queries, the record identifiers and digests, the cutoff and mode, the model version and whether the plan held across wordings. A view is saved in the person's private copy of the case and can be replayed; sharing it with other users requires the person to be signed in and is recorded as a decision.
  4. Mandatory parts counter omission. Blocking invented figures does not stop a model from leaving out an uncomfortable part. Each question kind in Table 8 has parts every view must show; the validator refuses a plan without them.
  5. Connecting text quotes, it does not compute. The model's text between components carries no numerals except record fields quoted by identifier.
  6. "Insufficient" is an answer. A question whose honest answer needs a relation that is not a record returns "insufficient" with the missing record named. The supported query scope is the catalogue; the design does not grow into free querying of the warehouse.

Two effects are hypotheses: that the stability badge lowers inappropriate reliance, and that a view built this way is auditable enough to serve story 12. Section 12 specifies the experiment that would test the design against a fixed dashboard and the narrowing that follows if it loses.

#11. Background and related work

AI and the content of work. Large language models are exposed to a large share of occupational tasks [Eloundou et al. 2024]. Controlled and field studies report productivity gains for writing [Noy and Zhang 2023] and customer support [Brynjolfsson et al. 2025], concentrated among less experienced workers, and a "jagged frontier" in consulting work, where AI helps inside the model's competence and hurts outside it [Dell'Acqua et al. 2026]. These studies measure the output and quality of tasks. They do not measure whether an organization can later reconstruct and review a decision taken with AI.

Reliance and trust. Levels of automation separate who proposes from who decides [Parasuraman, Sheridan and Wickens 2000]. Trust should match reliability; misuse and disuse are both failures [Lee and See 2004]. Automation bias and complacency are attentional effects that structure, not exhortation, must counter [Parasuraman and Manzey 2010]. Human–AI teams often fail to outperform the better of the two alone, and explanations raise acceptance of advice whether or not it is right [Bansal et al. 2021].

Generative interfaces. Research systems generate interfaces from a task model the model builds from the prompt [Cao, Jiang and Xia 2025] or from the prompt directly, where raters preferred generated interfaces to text when generation time was ignored [Leviathan et al. 2026], and a consumer assistant began answering with charts, forms and maps in October 2026 [Ropek 2026]. These systems let the model write the interface and its content. Section 10 keeps the model's choice of layout and removes its authorship of any figure.

Accountability. Accountability is a relation in which an actor must explain and justify conduct to a forum that can question and judge [Bovens 2007]. Computing systems blur it through many hands, bugs and the computer as scapegoat [Nissenbaum 1996]. Internal audit frameworks call for documentation produced during development, not after it [Raji et al. 2020]. Regulation now requires automatic record-keeping and effective human oversight for high-risk AI systems [European Union 2024, Articles 12 and 14], and risk-management guidance names accountability and transparency as core properties [NIST 2023]. These sources say that records and oversight are required. We propose which records, with which time fields, make a decision answerable.

Belief, time and lineage. A truth maintenance system keeps a belief while its justifications hold [Doyle 1979]. Belief revision names expansion, revision and contraction as the moves on a belief set [Alchourrón, Gärdenfors and Makinson 1985]. Bitemporal data separates valid time from transaction time [Jensen and Snodgrass 1999; Snodgrass 2000]. The provenance data model gives a vocabulary for the lineage of entities, activities and agents [W3C 2013].

Decisions, forecasts and their value. Decision analysis prices information by what it changes in the decision [Howard 1966; Howard and Matheson 2005] and judges a decision apart from its outcome [Howard and Abbas 2016]. A forecast's consistency, quality and value are different properties [Murphy 1993]; proper scoring rules measure quality honestly [Gneiting and Raftery 2007]. Electricity price forecasting has strong simple benchmarks that new methods rarely beat when tested fairly [Weron 2014; Lago et al. 2021].

Organizational learning. Single-loop learning corrects an action; double-loop learning changes the governing assumptions and goals [Argyris and Schön 1978]. A scoring receipt with attribution feeds the single loop; a pre-specified check that can change what the product is feeds the double loop.

Position of this work. The four traditions answer four questions: what do we know and when should it change; what should we do and what is timely information worth; who may propose, accept and act; how do outcomes change beliefs and methods. Most AI-at-work systems instantiate one of them. We argue that answering for a decision needs all four at once, joined by one time contract, and that the join is a record schema.

#12. Evaluation plan

Each experiment names the number it produces and the pre-stated result that would refute or narrow a proposition.

The relabelling experiment, the first scored month and the reliance measurement carry the claims about work and trust. Until they run, every sentence about value or trust in this paper is a hypothesis.

#13. Discussion

#13.1 What the evidence supports

The records exist and are enforced in code: time fields on every signal and belief, versions that cannot be edited, a materiality line that must be stated first, a decision record bound to a version, and a scorecard that shows weak methods as weak. The operator's own schedule lags its advisories by hours where it reflects them and does not reflect most trips. The conditions present at any price interval can be listed with the time each was knowable and ranked as evidence without a causal claim, and the two worked cases show the hindsight, assumed and strict answers diverging. AI judgment can be structured so that every answer is cited, bounded and open to inspection, and a disagreement with a rule-based reading is kept, not hidden.

#13.2 What it does not yet support

It does not show that decisions improve, that analysts work faster, that out-of-date beliefs cause the costly misses, that any user relies on the system appropriately, or that a board or regulator can use the records. Answering for a decision is so far a design property, demonstrated on parts of the chain, never on one complete case.

#13.3 Why records first

Two observations support the ordering before any user study. First, the one place where the model and the rule-based reading disagree is exactly where a record matters: without the cutoff, the passages and the presentation forms, the disagreement could not be stated, let alone settled. Second, the most decision-relevant horizon has no demonstrated forecast skill: no claim is possible under the gate with the origins available. An organization will decide there on beliefs, so its beliefs need owners and times before any model is added.

#13.4 What the users change

The stories pull toward exploration and speed: one dashboard, alerts, questions in plain language. They also pull toward causes ("driving factor", "root causes") that a record cannot honestly supply. The design keeps the first through governed generative views and translates the second into a ranking of what was unusual, labelled as evidence, and an attribution to the organization's own beliefs. Whether users accept that translation is an open question for the walkthrough in Table 13.

#13.5 Generality

The problem formulation (Section 2), the record kinds (Section 9) and the generative-view rules (Section 10) name no electricity quantity except in their examples; the price and portfolio in Section 2 are one instance of a position and an impact. A budget, a capacity plan, a credit book or a care pathway has beliefs, versions, an impact in its own units, decisions and a later truth. What transfers is the time contract, the seven kinds and the invariants. What does not transfer without a settlement clock is proposition 5's test, the scored month; a domain without a clear later truth keeps propositions 1 to 4 testable and loses the review share.

#14. Limitations and threats to validity

  • Author-written labels. The compiled-judgment comparison uses labels written by the authors; the relabelling experiment exists to replace them.
  • Two inference runs. Two model calls on one case cannot estimate accuracy.
  • Synthetic portfolio. Every portfolio-impact figure demonstrates a method; the 1,000,000-peso line is a policy of the demonstration, not the company's.
  • Data. Timestamps lack a recorded zone; pricing flags are inferred; three nodal months are missing; the projected schedules carry no run time, so their known-at times rest on assumption A-RUN; the fuel series rest on A-FUEL.
  • Settlement basis. Whether the price series is a retailer's true exposure is open.
  • No users yet. The requirements are stakeholder-supplied candidates, not observed use.
  • Self-evaluation. The prototype and its evaluations come from the same team. Each change was reviewed by a reviewer who did not write it, but there is no external replication, and no evaluator was blind to the hypothesis.
  • Selection. Case 1 was chosen because it was visible in the data, and case 2 by a stated rule (the highest-priced Visayas interval since March 2026 whose schedule rows arrived after the interval); neither is a sample.

#15. Ethical, authority and data boundaries

The prototype supports market intelligence and analyst decisions; it performs no grid control, issues no dispatch instruction and executes no trade. Market data is used under the signed permission record of Section 3.2. The public site shows a recorded replay and makes no live model call. AI output cannot grant a role or approve a change. The design aims to make human responsibility clearer, not to move it to a model: the person who accepts a belief or takes a decision is named in the record.

#16. Conclusion

When AI does the reading, the scarce thing is the answer to "who decided what, on which evidence, and when". We built a decision desk, InGRID, on a real market and measured what it can show today: an operator's schedule that lags its own advisories by hours where it reflects them and misses most trips, simple forecasts that are hard to beat beyond a day and untestable at the month horizon with the origins in hand, a card that lists the conditions present at any price with the time each was knowable and shows how the answer changes with what was known, and a recorded AI judgment whose one disagreement is kept. We named each model used and the role it fills. From these we framed AI-assisted decision work as an accountability problem to be designed before deployment, translated stakeholder-supplied stories into desk questions with mandatory parts, graded the factors the desk watches by what is a record, specified seven record kinds under one time contract and a governed form of generative interface, and reported what is measured and what is missing. The decisive tests, an analyst's blind relabelling, a generated view against a fixed dashboard, the first scored month and a measurement of reliance, are specified with the thresholds that would refute us.

The eight questions, their instruction text and their option sets. The instruction text below is the text the request carries, quoted from the question templates; the word "assumption" in the templates is the prototype's name for a belief. Each question is rendered in four presentation forms inside one request. Form 0 is the canonical text with the options in canonical order. Form 1 presents the options in a permuted order fixed by a digest of the task, rotated so that it never equals the canonical order; for a yes/no question, form 1 instead carries the negated text given in the table and its answer is un-negated before composition. Form 2 applies to the four choice questions only: it replaces each option label with a bracketed number ([1], [2], …) and appends "Answer with one bracketed option identifier." to the instruction; for the four yes/no questions form 2 is rendered identically to form 0, instructions and criteria alike, so those questions carry at most three distinct presentations and one repeat. In the run at the trip cutoff, which read one passage, the yes/no questions therefore had two distinct presentations and two repeats, and the choice questions three distinct presentations and one repeat; in the run at the return cutoff, which read four passages, the yes/no questions had three and the choice questions four. Thresholds (initial values, untuned; recorded in every receipt). Flip rate above 0.25 (tau_flip) or polarity gap above 0.3 (tau_pol) marks a question unstable. The rule thresholds tau_suff 0.6, tau_cond 0.5 and tau_lineage 0.5 are compared against the modal yes/no answer read as 1.0 or 0.0, so "Q7 at or above tau_suff" means the modal answer to Q7 is yes, and "Q4 below tau_cond" means it is no. A yes/no form's top is yes when its un-negated probability is at least 0.5; an even split across forms resolves to no (and, with four forms, is unstable under tau_flip before any rule reads it).

The fourteen composition rules, in order; the first that matches decides. "On an availability belief" means the belief's input field is an availability quantity in the model's representation.

  1. Any question unstable: insufficient.
  2. Q7 modal answer no (below tau_suff): insufficient.
  3. Q1 cannot tell: insufficient.
  4. Q1 names an entity other than the belief's: unrelated.
  5. Q2 other or grid alert: unrelated.
  6. Q5 modal answer yes (at or above tau_lineage): the label of the earlier source's judgment on the same belief; with none recorded, insufficient.
  7. Q2 commercial operation or return to service, Q3 same and Q4 no (below tau_cond), on an availability belief: supports.
